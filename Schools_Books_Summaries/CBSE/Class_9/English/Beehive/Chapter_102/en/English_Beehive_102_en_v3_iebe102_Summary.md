**Part II: The Shehnai of Bismillah Khan**

*   **Evolution of the Shehnai:** The Shehnai evolved from the harsh 'pungi'. A barber improved it, creating a melodious instrument played initially in the Shah's chambers ('Shah' + 'Nai' = Shehnai).
    *   *Diagram Idea 2: Pungi vs. Shehnai*

        |-----------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------|
        | Sound           | Shrill, unpleasant        | Soft, melodious             |
        | Structure       | Reeded noisemaker         | Natural hollow stem pipe    |
        | Length/Breadth  | Shorter, narrower         | Longer, broader             |
        | Holes           | Unspecified               | Seven holes                 |
        | Originator      | Generic                   | Barber in royal palace      |
        | Initial Context | Banned in royal residence | Played in Shah's chambers   |
*   **Bismillah Khan's Journey:** Born into a family of musicians in Dumraon, Bihar, he was fascinated by the Shehnai from a young age in Benaras (Varanasi). His maternal uncle, Ali Bux, mentored him. The banks of the Ganga and temples became his practice haunts.
*   **Elevating the Shehnai:** Ustad Bismillah Khan is credited with bringing the Shehnai from temples and weddings onto the classical music stage. His performance on India's independence (August 15, 1947) at the Red Fort was historic.
*   **National Icon and Cultural Roots:** Despite international fame and receiving India's highest civilian award, the **Bharat Ratna** (2001), Khansaab remained deeply connected to his roots in **Benaras (Varanasi)** and **Dumraon**, and the holy **Ganga**. His life exemplifies India's rich cultural heritage and communal harmony (a devout Muslim playing at the Kashi Vishwanath temple).

**Poem: Wind**

*   **Dual Nature of Wind:** The poem personifies wind, first addressing it as a destructive force that targets the weak ('crumbling houses', 'weaklings').
*   **Metaphor for Life's Challenges:** The wind symbolizes difficulties and adversities in life. These challenges break down the weak but make the strong even stronger.
*   **Call for Strength:** The poet urges readers to build strength – physically ('strong homes', 'firm body') and mentally/emotionally ('steadfast heart') – to withstand life's challenges.
*   **Befriending Adversity:** By becoming strong, one can not only withstand but also befriend the 'wind' (challenges), turning it into a source of strength ('makes strong fires roar and flourish'). The message is one of resilience and proactive self-strengthening.

*   **National Recognition:** Ustad Bismillah Khan received India's highest civilian honour, the **Bharat Ratna** (2001), along with other prestigious national awards like the **Padma Vibhushan**, **Padma Bhushan**, and **Padmashri**, signifying his immense contribution to Indian culture.
*   **Cultural Syncretism:** Bismillah Khan's life, being a devout Muslim who played the Shehnai daily at the **Kashi Vishwanath temple** in **Varanasi (Benaras)**, exemplifies India's unique **Ganga-Jamuni Tehzeeb** (syncretic culture). His deep love for Varanasi and the **River Ganga** underscores the profound connection many Indians feel towards their homeland and cultural landmarks.
*   **Literary Context:** The poem 'Wind' is by **Subramania Bharati**, a great Tamil poet known for his patriotic and reformist writings during India's pre-Independence era. The translation by **A.K. Ramanujan**, another renowned Indian poet and scholar, makes this powerful Tamil work accessible to a wider audience.
