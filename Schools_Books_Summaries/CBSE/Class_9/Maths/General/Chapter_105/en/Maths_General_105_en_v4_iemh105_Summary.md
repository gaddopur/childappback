        *   Thales (c. 640-546 BCE): Credited with the first proof (diameter bisects circle).
        *   Pythagoras (c. 572 BCE): Developed geometric properties and theory.
3.  **Euclid's Contribution (c. 300 BCE):**
    *   Systematisation: Collected known work in 'Elements' (13 books).
    *   Axiomatic Approach: Based on definitions, axioms, and postulates.
4.  **Euclid's Building Blocks:**
    *   **Undefined Terms:** Recognition that some terms cannot be defined without circularity (e.g., point, line, plane). Based on intuitive understanding derived from physical world (Solid -> Surface -> Line -> Point).
    *   **Definitions:** Attempted definitions for concepts (e.g., point has no part, line is breadthless length). 23 definitions in Book 1.
    *   **Axioms (Common Notions):** Obvious universal truths, assumed true without proof, used throughout mathematics.
        *   Equality principles (Things equal to the same thing..., If equals added/subtracted..., Things coinciding...)
        *   Whole-Part relationship (Whole > Part)
        *   Operations on equals (Doubles/Halves of same things...)
    *   **Postulates:** Assumptions specific to geometry, assumed true without proof.
        *   Postulate 1: Drawing a straight line between two points. (Supplemented by Axiom 5.1: Uniqueness)
        *   Postulate 2: Extending a terminated line (line segment) indefinitely.
        *   Postulate 3: Drawing a circle with any center and radius.
        *   Postulate 4: All right angles are equal.
        *   Postulate 5: Parallel line condition (If interior angles sum < 180°, lines meet). Noted for its complexity.
5.  **Theorems (Propositions):**
    *   Statements proved logically using definitions, axioms, postulates, and previously proved theorems.
    *   Example: Theorem 5.1 - Two distinct lines cannot have more than one point in common.
6.  **Deductive Reasoning:** The process of proving theorems based on the established axioms and postulates.

## 📘 Key Learnings

**1. The Origins and Evolution of Geometry:**
Geometry arose from practical needs like measuring land, constructing buildings, and calculating volumes in ancient civilizations like Egypt, Babylonia, India, and China. While early geometry (e.g., Egypt, India) was often practical and results-oriented, the Greeks, notably Thales and Pythagoras, emphasized logical reasoning and proving *why* geometric statements were true.

**2. Euclid's Axiomatic System:**
Euclid, in his work 'Elements', organized geometry systematically. His approach forms the foundation of classical geometry. He started with fundamental concepts:
*   **Abstract Notions:** Moving from physical objects (solids) to abstract ideas (surfaces, lines, points), losing one dimension at each step.
*   **The Problem of Definitions:** Euclid provided definitions (e.g., "A point is that which has no part"), but defining fundamental terms like 'part' leads to an endless chain. Therefore, modern mathematics accepts basic terms like **point, line, and plane** as **undefined**, relying on intuition and physical models.
📈 **Diagrammatic Concept:** Solid (3D) → Surface (2D, boundary of solid) → Line (1D, edge of surface) → Point (0D, end of line).

**3. Axioms vs. Postulates:**
Euclid distinguished between two types of assumptions:
*   **Axioms (Common Notions):** Self-evident truths applicable across all mathematics, not just geometry. Examples:
    *   "Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another." (If Area(A)=Area(B) and Area(B)=Area(C), then Area(A)=Area(C)).
    *   "If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal." (If x=y, then x+2 = y+2).
    *   "The whole is greater than the part." (If A = B + C, where B and C are positive quantities, then A > B).
*   **Postulates:** Assumptions specific to geometry. Euclid listed five:
    1.  **Joining Points:** A straight line can be drawn between any two points. (Axiom 5.1 clarifies this line is *unique*).
    2.  **Extending Lines:** A terminated line (line segment) can be extended indefinitely.
    📈 **Diagram:** `A-----B` can become `...---A-----B---...`
    3.  **Drawing Circles:** A circle can be drawn with any point as center and any length as radius.
    4.  **Right Angles:** All right angles are equal to each other (establishing a standard measure).
    5.  **Parallel Postulate:** If a line intersects two other lines, making the sum of interior angles on one side less than two right angles (180°), then the two lines, if extended indefinitely, will meet on that side. This postulate is notably more complex than the others and is equivalent to the statement that through a point not on a given line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn.
    📈 **Diagram (Fig 5.6):** If ∠1 + ∠2 < 180°, lines AB and CD meet on the left.

**4. Theorems and Proofs:**
Statements that are *proved* using definitions, axioms, postulates, and deductive reasoning are called **theorems** or **propositions**. Euclid derived 465 propositions in 'Elements'.
*   **Example Proof (Theorem 5.1):** *Two distinct lines cannot have more than one point in common.*
    *   *Proof by Contradiction:* Assume two distinct lines *l* and *m* intersect at two distinct points P and Q. This implies two different lines pass through the same two points (P and Q). But this contradicts Axiom 5.1 (Given two distinct points, there is a *unique* line that passes through them). Therefore, the initial assumption must be false. Conclusion: Two distinct lines can intersect at *at most* one point.

**5. Consistency:**
An axiomatic system (like Euclid's) must be **consistent**, meaning it should be impossible to deduce contradictory statements from the axioms and postulates.

## 🌏 Bharatiya Context

Geometry in ancient India (Bharat) had significant developments, particularly linked to practical and religious needs:

1.  **Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3000 BCE):**
    *   Excavations at Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro reveal advanced urban planning.
    *   **Evidence:** Roads were laid out parallel to each other, intersecting at right angles (grid pattern). Sophisticated underground drainage systems existed. Houses had multiple rooms.
    *   **Implication:** This demonstrates practical knowledge of geometry, mensuration (measurement of lengths, areas), and arithmetic.
    *   **Standardisation:** Kiln-fired bricks were used with dimensions consistently in the ratio **Length : Breadth : Thickness = 4 : 2 : 1**.

2.  **Vedic Period - Sulbasutras (c. 800 BCE - 500 BCE):**
    *   These texts were manuals for geometrical constructions, primarily for building **altars (vedis)** and fireplaces for Vedic rituals.
    *   **Purpose:** The effectiveness of rituals was believed to depend on the precise shape and area of the altars.
    *   **Constructions:** Included methods for constructing squares, circles, rectangles, triangles, and trapeziums. Also involved constructing shapes with specific area requirements (e.g., a square with the same area as a given circle, or vice versa).
    *   **Examples:** Square and circular altars for household rituals; combinations of rectangles, triangles, and trapeziums for public worship altars.
    *   **Note:** While accurate geometric methods were used, the underlying mathematical principles or proofs were generally not discussed in these texts, indicating a focus on procedural knowledge.

3.  **Sriyantra:**
    *   Mentioned in the Atharvaveda, this complex geometric figure consists of nine interwoven isosceles triangles arranged to form 43 subsidiary triangles. It showcases intricate knowledge of geometric properties and construction.

These examples highlight that geometry was actively developed and applied in ancient India, particularly for practical purposes like construction, town planning, and religious rituals, contributing significantly to the global history of mathematics.
