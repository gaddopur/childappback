*   **Natural Numbers (N):** Counting numbers {1, 2, 3, ...}. Infinitely many.
*   **Whole Numbers (W):** Natural numbers including zero {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}.
*   **Integers (Z):** Whole numbers and their negatives {..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ...}. (Z from German 'Zahlen' - to count).
*   **Rational Numbers (Q):** Numbers expressible as p/q, where p, q are integers, q ≠ 0. Includes N, W, Z. (Q from 'Quotient'). Represented uniquely in co-prime form. Infinitely many rationals exist between any two rationals.
*   **Irrational Numbers:** Numbers that *cannot* be expressed as p/q. Examples: √2, √3, π, 0.101101110...
*   **Real Numbers (R):** The collection of *all* rational and irrational numbers. Every point on the number line corresponds to a unique real number, and vice versa.

## 📘 Key Learnings

**1. Identifying Number Types:**
*   **Rational (Q):** Can be written as p/q, q≠0. Integers (like -3 = -3/1), fractions (like 3/4), terminating decimals (0.5 = 1/2), non-terminating recurring decimals (0.333... = 1/3).
*   **Irrational:** Cannot be written as p/q. Non-terminating, non-recurring decimals (√2 = 1.41421..., π = 3.14159..., 0.121221222...).

**2. Decimal Expansions:**
*   **Rational Numbers:** Have decimal expansions that are either:
    *   **Terminating:** End after a finite number of digits (e.g., 7/8 = 0.875). Occurs when the denominator 'q' (in simplest form p/q) has prime factors only 2 and/or 5. (Evaluate: Can you determine if 3/40 has a terminating decimal without dividing?)
    *   **Non-terminating Recurring:** Go on forever with a repeating block of digits (e.g., 10/3 = 3.333... = 3.3̅; 1/7 = 0.142857142857... = 0.1̅4̅2̅8̅5̅7̅). The number of digits in the repeating block is always less than the divisor 'q'.
*   **Irrational Numbers:** Have decimal expansions that are **Non-terminating and Non-recurring**.

**3. Representing Numbers on the Number Line:**
*   Rational numbers are easily placed.
*   Irrational numbers like √2, √3 can be located geometrically using the Pythagoras theorem.
    *   **Locating √n:** Construct a square root spiral or use successive right-angled triangles. (See Fig 1.7, 1.8 in NCERT).
    *   **Locating √x (for any positive real x):** Geometric construction involving a semicircle (See Fig 1.11, 1.12 in NCERT).

    1. Mark AB = 3.5 units. Mark BC = 1 unit.
    2. Find midpoint O of AC. Draw semicircle with center O, radius OC.
    3. Draw BD perpendicular to AC. BD = √3.5.
    4. With center B, radius BD, draw arc to intersect the line at E. E represents √3.5.

**4. Operations on Real Numbers:**
*   Rational + Rational = Rational
*   Rational × Rational = Rational
*   Rational + Irrational = **Irrational**
*   Rational (≠0) × Irrational = **Irrational**
*   Irrational + Irrational = May be Rational (e.g., √2 + (-√2) = 0) or Irrational (e.g., √2 + √3)
*   Irrational × Irrational = May be Rational (e.g., √2 × √2 = 2) or Irrational (e.g., √2 × √3 = √6)

**5. Identities for Positive Real Numbers (a, b > 0):**
*   √(ab) = √a √b
*   √(a/b) = √a / √b
*   (√a + √b)(√a - √b) = a - b
*   (a + √b)(a - √b) = a² - b
*   (√a + √b)² = a + 2√(ab) + b

**6. Rationalizing the Denominator:**
*   The process of converting an expression with an irrational denominator to an equivalent expression with a rational denominator.
*   Uses identities, especially conjugates (e.g., the conjugate of √a + √b is √a - √b).
*   Example: Rationalize 1/(√7 - √6)
    *   Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate (√7 + √6):
        1/(√7 - √6) * (√7 + √6)/(√7 + √6) = (√7 + √6) / (7 - 6) = √7 + √6

**7. Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers (a > 0, p, q are rational):**
*   aᵖ ⋅ a<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9> = aᵖ⁺<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9>
*   (aᵖ)<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9> = aᵖ<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9>
*   aᵖ / a<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9> = aᵖ⁻<0xE1><0xB5><0xA9>
*   aᵖ bᵖ = (ab)ᵖ
*   a⁰ = 1
*   a⁻ᵖ = 1/aᵖ
*   ⁿ√a = a¹/ⁿ

## 🌏 Bharatiya Context

While the core concepts are universal, mathematics in India has a rich history contributing to Number Systems:

1.  **Approximation of √2:** The ancient Indian mathematical texts, **Sulbasutras** (Vedic period, ~800-500 BC), provided a remarkably accurate approximation for √2:
    √2 ≈ 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3×4) - 1/(3×4×34) ≈ 1.4142156...
    This demonstrates early Indian engagement with irrational quantities, particularly in the context of geometric constructions (like altars). Evaluate the accuracy of this approximation compared to the modern value (√2 ≈ 1.41421356...).
2.  **Value of π:** The great Indian mathematician and astronomer **Aryabhatta** (476–550 CE) calculated the value of π correct to four decimal places as **3.1416**. This was a significant achievement for its time and widely used. Compare this to Archimedes' bounds mentioned in the text.
3.  **Concept of Zero and Place Value:** While not explicitly detailed in this chapter's text, the invention of zero ( शून्य / Shunya) and the decimal place value system in India were foundational for the development and representation of the entire number system we use today, including rational and irrational numbers.
