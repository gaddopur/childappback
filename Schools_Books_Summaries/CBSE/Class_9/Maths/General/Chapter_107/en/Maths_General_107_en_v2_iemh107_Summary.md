**Hierarchy:**
1.  **Triangle Basics:** Definition (3 sides, 3 angles, 3 vertices).
2.  **Congruence:** General concept (same shape and size), applied to triangles.
    *   **Notation:** ∆ ABC ≅ ∆ PQR (implies specific correspondence A↔P, B↔Q, C↔R).
    *   **CPCT:** Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are equal.
3.  **Criteria for Congruence:** Minimum conditions to prove triangles congruent.
    *   **SAS Axiom:** Two sides and the *included* angle.
    *   **ASA Theorem:** Two angles and the *included* side.
    *   **AAS Rule:** Two angles and one *non-included* corresponding side.
    *   **SSS Theorem:** All three corresponding sides.
    *   **RHS Theorem:** In right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one corresponding side.
    *   **Important Note:** SSA/ASS is *not* a valid congruence criterion. AAA is also *not* sufficient for congruence.
4.  **Properties of Triangles:** Specific characteristics based on sides/angles.
    *   **Isosceles Triangle:** A triangle with two equal sides.
        *   Theorem 7.2: Angles opposite equal sides are equal.
        *   Theorem 7.3 (Converse): Sides opposite equal angles are equal.
    *   **Equilateral Triangle:** All sides equal; consequently, all angles are 60°.

## 📘 Key Learnings

**1. Concept of Congruence:**
   - Two geometric figures are congruent if they have the same shape and size. They cover each other exactly when superimposed.
   - Examples: Two coins of the same denomination minted in the same year, two ATM cards from the same bank, two squares of the same side length.
   - For triangles, congruence means corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal.
   - **Correspondence:** The order of vertices in the congruence notation (e.g., ∆ ABC ≅ ∆ PQR) is crucial. It defines the one-to-one correspondence between vertices (A↔P, B↔Q, C↔R) and hence, the corresponding equal sides (AB=PQ, BC=QR, CA=RP) and angles (∠A=∠P, ∠B=∠Q, ∠C=∠R).

**2. Criteria for Triangle Congruence:**

   *   **SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Axiom 7.1:** If two sides and the *included* angle of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two sides and the *included* angle of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* Imagine two triangles where side AB = PQ, side BC = QR, and the angle *between* them, ∠B = ∠Q. These triangles must be congruent. (Ref: Fig 7.7)

   *   **ASA (Angle-Side-Angle) Theorem 7.1:** If two angles and the *included* side of one triangle are equal to the corresponding two angles and the *included* side of another triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* Imagine two triangles where ∠B = ∠E, ∠C = ∠F, and the side *between* them, BC = EF. These triangles must be congruent. (Ref: Fig 7.11, 7.12)

   *   **AAS (Angle-Angle-Side) Congruence Rule:** If any two pairs of angles and one pair of corresponding sides are equal, the triangles are congruent. (This follows from ASA because if two angles are equal, the third angle must also be equal by the angle sum property).
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* Imagine two triangles where ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, and a non-included side BC = EF. These triangles are congruent.

   *   **SSS (Side-Side-Side) Theorem 7.4:** If the three sides of one triangle are equal to the corresponding three sides of another triangle, then the two triangles are congruent.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* Imagine two triangles where AB = DE, BC = EF, and CA = FD. These triangles must be congruent. (Ref: Fig 7.35)

   *   **RHS (Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side) Theorem 7.5:** If in two right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are equal to the hypotenuse and the corresponding side of the other triangle, then the triangles are congruent.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* Imagine two right triangles (∠B = ∠E = 90°) where hypotenuse AC = DF and one side AB = DE. These triangles are congruent. (Ref: Fig 7.36)

**3. Properties of Isosceles Triangles:**

   *   **Theorem 7.2:** Angles opposite to equal sides of an isosceles triangle are equal.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* In ∆ABC, if AB = AC, then ∠C = ∠B. (Ref: Fig 7.25)
   *   **Theorem 7.3 (Converse of 7.2):** The sides opposite to equal angles of a triangle are equal.
      *   *Diagrammatic Concept:* In ∆ABC, if ∠B = ∠C, then AC = AB. (Ref: Fig 7.26)

**4. CPCT (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles):**
   - Once two triangles are proven congruent using any of the above criteria, we can conclude that their remaining corresponding parts (sides and angles) are also equal. This is abbreviated as 'CPCT'. This is a powerful tool for proving equality of sides or angles.

## 🌏 Bharatiya Context

*   **Standardization in Manufacturing (BIS):** The concept of congruence is fundamental to standardization. The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) sets standards for various products, ensuring that components manufactured by different units, or batches produced at different times, are congruent (within acceptable tolerances). For example, standardized congruent floor tiles ensure seamless patterns and easy replacement. This promotes economic efficiency and quality assurance in Indian industries.
*   **Architectural Symmetry and Patterns:** Many traditional Indian architectural marvels, like temple gopurams, mosque minarets, or intricate 'jaali' (lattice screens) work, utilize congruent geometric shapes, especially triangles, to create complex symmetrical patterns. The repetition of congruent units provides aesthetic beauty and structural stability. Consider the congruent triangular motifs in the design of structures like the Taj Mahal or Humayun's Tomb.
*   **Textile Designs and Crafts:** Traditional Indian textiles, such as Phulkari embroidery from Punjab or Ikat weaving patterns, often employ repeating congruent triangular motifs. Artisans meticulously create these patterns, where the congruence of shapes contributes to the overall harmony and visual appeal of the fabric. This reflects the application of geometric principles in indigenous art forms.
