## 🌟 Core Concepts

This chapter delves into the critical issue of Food Security in India. The core concepts are structured hierarchically:

1.  **Understanding Food Security**
    *   Definition: Availability, accessibility, and affordability of food for all people at all times.
    *   Dimensions:
        *   *Availability:* Food production, imports, and government buffer stocks.
        *   *Accessibility:* Food within physical reach of everyone.
        *   *Affordability:* Individuals having enough money to buy sufficient, safe, and nutritious food.
    *   Evolution of the Concept: From mere availability (UN 1975) to include access and entitlements (Amartya Sen) and nutritional aspects (World Food Summit 1995).
    *   Necessity: Essential for living, especially crucial during calamities (drought, flood, earthquake, pandemic like Covid-19) and for the poorest sections.

2.  **Vulnerability to Food Insecurity**
    *   Impact of Calamities: Decreased production, shortage, price rise, starvation, potential famine (e.g., Bengal Famine 1943).
    *   Identifying Food-Insecure Groups:
        *   *Rural:* Landless labourers, small farmers, traditional artisans, petty self-employed, destitutes.
        *   *Urban:* Casual labourers, workers in ill-paid occupations, seasonal workers (e.g., construction).
        *   *Social Composition:* SCs, STs, some OBC sections with poor land base.
        *   *Specific Demographics:* Women (especially pregnant/nursing), children under 5.
        *   *Geographical:* Economically backward states (UP, Bihar, Jharkhand, Orissa, WB, Chhattisgarh, MP, Maharashtra), tribal/remote areas, disaster-prone regions.
    *   Hunger as an Indicator:
        *   *Chronic Hunger:* Persistent inadequacy of diet (quantity/quality) due to low income.
        *   *Seasonal Hunger:* Related to agricultural cycles (rural) or casual work availability (urban).

3.  **India's Food Security System**
    *   Goal: Self-sufficiency in foodgrains (post-Independence).
    *   Green Revolution: Strategy adopted (especially wheat and rice), leading to increased production and avoiding widespread famine. Recorded impressive growth, particularly in states like Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh.
    *   Government System Components:
        *   **Buffer Stock:**
            *   Managed by: Food Corporation of India (FCI).
            *   Process: FCI procures wheat and rice at Minimum Support Price (MSP) from surplus states.
            *   MSP: Pre-announced price to incentivize farmers.
            *   Purpose: Distribute foodgrains in deficit areas/among poor at subsidized rates (Issue Price); stabilize prices; ensure availability during calamities.
        *   **Public Distribution System (PDS):**
            *   Mechanism: Distribution of procured foodgrains through government-regulated ration shops (Fair Price Shops).
            *   Reach: Approx. 5.5 lakh shops across India.
            *   Items: Foodgrains (wheat, rice), sugar, kerosene at prices lower than market rates.
            *   Ration Cards: Entitlement based on category (Antyodaya, BPL, APL).
            *   Evolution: Universal (pre-1992) -> Revamped PDS (RPDS, 1992) -> Targeted PDS (TPDS, 1997) -> Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY, 2000) -> Annapurna Scheme (APS, 2000) -> National Food Security Act (NFSA, 2013).
            *   NFSA, 2013: Legal entitlement to subsidized foodgrains for ~75% rural and ~50% urban population.

4.  **Challenges and Critiques of the System**
    *   Buffer Stock Issues: Overflowing granaries, high carrying costs, wastage, grain deterioration, pressure from surplus states leading to skewed procurement (wheat/rice focus).
    *   MSP Issues: Induced shift from coarse grains to wheat/rice, environmental degradation (water depletion), rising procurement/maintenance costs.
    *   PDS Issues: Malpractices by dealers (diversion, poor quality grains, irregular opening), unsold poor quality stocks, declining incentive for APL families due to near-market prices.
    *   Impact on Consumption: Trends show changes in per capita consumption of rice and wheat, with increased reliance on PDS grains.

5.  **Role of Cooperatives and NGOs**
    *   Cooperatives: Significant role, especially in southern/western India.
        *   Examples: Fair price shops in Tamil Nadu, Mother Dairy (Delhi - milk/vegetables), Amul (Gujarat - milk products, White Revolution).
    *   NGOs: Facilitating food security initiatives.
        *   Example: Academy of Development Science (ADS) in Maharashtra facilitating NGO networks for Grain Banks, providing training and influencing policy.

## 📘 Key Learnings

1.  **Food Security is Multi-dimensional:** It's not just about having enough food produced (**Availability**), but also ensuring people can physically get it (**Accessibility**) and have the money to buy sufficient, nutritious food (**Affordability**). Lack in any dimension leads to insecurity.
    *   *Diagram Idea:* A triangle with vertices labelled Availability, Accessibility, Affordability, with "Food Security" in the center.

2.  **Vulnerability Varies:** While poverty is a major cause, even those above the poverty line can become food insecure during natural disasters or economic shocks (like pandemics). Specific groups like landless labourers, casual workers, certain social groups (SC/ST/OBCs), women, and children are disproportionately affected. Certain states in India face higher levels of food insecurity.
    *   *Example:* The Bengal Famine of 1943 illustrates how a calamity, combined with other factors, can lead to mass starvation, affecting labourers and the poor most severely, even if overall production figures weren't drastically low compared to previous years (refer Table 4.1 analysis).

3.  **India's Strategy - Green Revolution & Buffer Stock/PDS:** India aimed for self-sufficiency post-Independence, largely achieved through the Green Revolution which significantly boosted wheat and rice production. The government maintains food security through:
    *   **Buffer Stock:** Procuring grains (wheat/rice) via FCI at MSP to ensure supply and stabilize prices.
    *   **PDS:** Distributing these grains at subsidized rates (Issue Price) through ration shops.
    *   *Flowchart Idea:* Farmer -> (MSP) -> FCI Procurement -> Buffer Stock (Granaries) -> PDS (Ration Shops) -> (Issue Price/Subsidized Rate) -> Consumer.

4.  **Evolution of PDS:** The PDS has evolved from a universal scheme to a targeted one (TPDS) focusing on the poor, with specific schemes like AAY for the "poorest of the poor" and the overarching NFSA, 2013, which legally entitles a large portion of the population to subsidized food.
    *   *Timeline Idea:* Universal PDS -> RPDS (1992) -> TPDS (1997) -> AAY/APS (2000) -> NFSA (2013).

5.  **Systemic Challenges:** Despite successes, the system faces issues:
    *   High buffer stocks lead to waste and high costs.
    *   MSP focus on wheat/rice affects crop diversity and sustainability (e.g., water levels in Punjab/Haryana).
    *   PDS suffers from inefficiencies and malpractices.

6.  **Role Beyond Government:** Cooperatives (like Amul, Mother Dairy) and NGOs (like ADS setting up Grain Banks) play a vital role in strengthening food security, particularly at regional and community levels.
