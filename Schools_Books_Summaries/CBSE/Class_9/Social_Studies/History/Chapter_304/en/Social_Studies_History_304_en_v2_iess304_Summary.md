# [Class 9] History - Chapter 304: Forest Society and Colonialism

## 🌟 Core Concepts

This chapter explores the profound impact of colonialism on forest ecosystems and the lives of communities dependent on them, primarily focusing on India and Java (Indonesia).

1.  **The Importance of Forests:**
    *   Source of diverse resources (timber, fuel, food, medicines, materials like rubber, bamboo).
    *   Habitat for numerous plant and animal species (biodiversity).
    *   Livelihood source for various communities (forest dwellers, pastoralists, shifting cultivators).

2.  **Deforestation under Colonial Rule:**
    *   **Definition:** The disappearance of forests. While not new, it became systematic and extensive under colonialism.
    *   **Causes:**
        *   **Agricultural Expansion:** Belief that cultivation signified progress and generated revenue; clearing forests for farming, encouraged by the colonial state (e.g., 6.7 million hectares increase in India, 1880-1920). Encouragement of commercial crops (jute, sugar, wheat, cotton).
        *   **Shipbuilding:** Depletion of oak forests in England led to sourcing timber (like Teak, Sal) from colonies like India for the Royal Navy.
        *   **Railways:** Massive demand for timber for fuel and especially **sleepers** (wooden planks holding tracks) – requiring 1,760-2,000 sleepers per mile. Led to indiscriminate felling by contractors.
        *   **Plantations:** Clearing natural forests for large-scale tea, coffee, and rubber plantations, often granted cheaply to European planters.

3.  **The Rise of 'Scientific' Forestry:**
    *   **Objective:** Control and manage forest resources primarily for timber production (meeting colonial needs).
    *   **Implementation (India):**
        *   Appointment of Dietrich Brandis (German expert) as first Inspector General of Forests.
        *   Establishment of the Indian Forest Service (1864).
        *   Enactment of Forest Acts (1865, amended 1878, 1927).
        *   Establishment of the Imperial Forest Research Institute (Dehradun, 1906).
    *   **Methodology:**
        *   Surveying forests, estimating resources, creating working plans.
        *   Replacing diverse natural forests with **plantations** (single species like teak or sal, planted in rows).
        *   **Forest Classification (Act of 1878):**
            *   *Reserved Forests:* Best forests; villagers denied access.
            *   *Protected Forests:* Limited access for fuel/house building.
            *   *Village Forests:* Limited access for local use.
    *   **Critique:** Ecologically unsound (loss of biodiversity), disregarded local needs and knowledge.

4.  **Impact on Local Communities:**
    *   **Loss of Livelihood:** Everyday practices (collecting fuelwood, fodder, fruits, roots; grazing cattle; hunting; fishing) became illegal. Led to hardship, dependence on forest guards (bribery), theft.
    *   **Effect on Cultivation:** Ban on **shifting cultivation** (swidden agriculture - local names: *jhum, podu, bewar* etc.) viewed as harmful to timber production and difficult to tax. Led to displacement and hardship for communities like the Baigas of Central India.
    *   **Hunting Restrictions:** Customary hunting rights prohibited; hunting became a sport for colonial officials and Indian nobility, leading to near-extinction of species like tigers.
    *   **New, Exploitative Systems:**
        *   Forced labour for forest department (e.g., 'forest villages' in Bastar).
        *   Displacement of nomadic/pastoralist communities (e.g., Korava, Karacha, Yerukula in Madras Presidency), sometimes branded 'criminal tribes'.
        *   Recruitment into plantations (e.g., Santhals, Oraons, Gonds in Assam) with low wages and poor conditions.
        *   Trade regulated, benefiting European firms over traditional traders like Banjaras.

5.  **Rebellion and Resistance:**
    *   **India (Bastar Rebellion, 1910):**
        *   *Context:* Southern Chhattisgarh; diverse communities (Maria, Muria Gonds, Dhurwas, Bhatras, Halbas).
        *   *Causes:* Forest reservation proposal (1905), ban on shifting cultivation/hunting/collection, increased rents, demands for free labour (*begar*), famines (1899-1900, 1907-1908).
        *   *Initiators:* Dhurwas of Kanger forest; key figure Gunda Dhur.
        *   *Methods:* Circulation of messages (mango boughs, earth, chillies, arrows), looting bazaars, attacking symbols of colonial authority (police stations, schools, officials' homes).
        *   *Suppression:* British troops deployed; leaders attempted negotiation but were fired upon; villages punished.
        *   *Outcome:* Reservation temporarily suspended, area reduced; Gunda Dhur never captured.
    *   **Indonesia (Java - Samin's Challenge):**
        *   *Context:* Dutch colonial rule, demand for teak for ships/railways.
        *   *Dutch Policies:* Forest laws restricting access, introduction of 'scientific forestry', forced labour system (*blandongdiensten*).
        *   *Resistance:* Kalang community (skilled forest cutters) resisted in 1770. Surontiko Samin (Randublatung village, c. 1890) questioned state ownership of natural resources (wind, water, earth, wood).
        *   *Methods (Saminists):* Non-cooperation - refusing to pay taxes/fines, perform labour; protesting surveys by lying on land. By 1907, 3000 families involved.

6.  **Forests and Wars:**
    *   World Wars led to abandonment of working plans and reckless felling to meet war needs (India).
    *   Dutch used 'scorched earth' policy in Java before Japanese occupation, destroying timber stocks. Japanese exploited forests further.
    *   Post-war, villagers expanded cultivation into forests, leading to conflict with forest departments trying to reclaim land.

7.  **New Developments in Forestry:**
    *   Since the 1980s, recognition that excluding communities leads to conflict.
    *   Shift towards **conservation** over timber extraction.
    *   Emphasis on involving local communities in forest management.
    *   Recognition of traditional conservation practices (e.g., **sacred groves** - *sarnas, devarakudu, kan, rai* in India).
    *   Community patrolling and alternative forest management models emerging.

## 📘 Key Learnings

1.  **Colonialism fundamentally altered forest use:** Forests, traditionally managed by local communities for diverse needs (fuel, fodder, food, materials), were transformed into state-controlled resources primarily for timber extraction to serve colonial economic and strategic interests (shipbuilding, railways).

2.  **'Scientific Forestry' was driven by colonial needs, not ecological science:** This system prioritized commercially valuable timber species (like Teak and Sal in India) grown in monoculture plantations, destroying biodiversity and ignoring the multifaceted needs of local populations who relied on mixed forests.
    *   *Diagram Idea 1: Comparison Table*
        | Feature             | Traditional Forest Use                     | 'Scientific' Forestry (Colonial)        |
        | :------------------ | :----------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------- |
        | **Goal**            | Diverse needs (fuel, fodder, food, etc.) | Timber production (commercial)          |
        | **Species**         | Mixed, diverse species                     | Monoculture (Teak, Sal, Pine etc.)      |
        | **Management**      | Community-based, customary rules         | State-controlled (Forest Dept.), Acts |
        | **Access**          | Generally open to local communities        | Restricted (Reserved, Protected areas)  |
        | **Ecological Impact** | Sustainable (often), maintained diversity | Loss of biodiversity, soil degradation |
        | **Social Impact**   | Supported local livelihoods                | Displaced communities, conflict         |

3.  **Forest Laws criminalized traditional livelihoods:** Acts like the Indian Forest Act (1878) classified forests and severely restricted access, turning age-old practices like collecting forest produce, grazing, hunting, and shifting cultivation into illegal activities, leading to immense hardship and conflict.

4.  **Deforestation accelerated due to specific colonial demands:** The need for timber for the Royal Navy, the massive expansion of railways requiring millions of sleepers, and the clearing of land for revenue-generating agriculture and plantations were key drivers.

5.  **Resistance took various forms:** Communities did not passively accept these changes. Resistance ranged from everyday acts (like 'stealing' wood) to organized, large-scale rebellions (Bastar) and ideological challenges to state ownership (Saminism in Java).

6.  **Colonial forest policies had parallels across regions:** Both British India and Dutch Java saw the implementation of restrictive forest laws, 'scientific' forestry focused on timber, exploitation of local labour, and subsequent resistance from affected communities.

7.  **Modern conservation increasingly recognizes the role of local communities:** The failures and conflicts generated by exclusionary state-centric forestry have led to a gradual shift towards involving forest communities in conservation and management, acknowledging the effectiveness of traditional practices like sacred groves.

## 🌏 Bharatiya Context

This chapter is rich with India-specific examples and data illustrating the impact of colonial forest policies:

1.  **Institutions & Laws:**
    *   **Dietrich Brandis:** First Inspector General of Forests in India.
    *   **Indian Forest Service:** Established in 1864.
    *   **Indian Forest Acts:** 1865, 1878 (classified forests into Reserved, Protected, Village), 1927.
    *   **Imperial Forest Research Institute:** Set up in Dehradun (1906).

2.  **Economic & Environmental Data:**
    *   **Agricultural Expansion:** Cultivated area rose from approx. 1/6th to 1/2 of India's landmass over centuries; increased by 6.7 million hectares between 1880-1920 alone.
    *   **Railway Impact:** By 1890, 25,500 km of track; over 765,000 km by 1946. Each mile needed 1,760-2,000 sleepers. In Madras Presidency (1850s), 35,000 trees cut annually for sleepers.
    *   **Forest Cover Decline:** Between 1880-1920, forest cover declined by 9.7 million hectares (from 108.6 to 98.9 million hectares).
    *   **Hunting:** 1875-1925: Rewards given for killing over 80,000 tigers, 150,000 leopards, 200,000 wolves. Maharaja of Sarguja shot 1,157 tigers.

3.  **Specific Locations & Communities:**
    *   **Regions:** Bastar (Chhattisgarh), Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), Santhal Parganas (Jharkhand), Andhra Pradesh, Madras Presidency, Assam.
    *   **Communities:** Maria Gonds, Muria Gonds, Dhurwas, Bhatras, Halbas (Bastar); Baigas (Central India); Korava, Karacha, Yerukula (Madras Presidency); Santhals, Oraons, Gonds (recruited for Assam tea plantations); Banjaras (nomadic traders).
    *   **Rebellion Leaders:** Siddhu and Kanu (Santhal Parganas), Birsa Munda (Chhotanagpur), Alluri Sitarama Raju (Andhra Pradesh), Gunda Dhur (Bastar).

4.  **Forest Products & Trees:**
    *   **Timber:** Teak, Sal (promoted by Forest Dept.).
    *   **Other Products:** Tendu leaves (for *bidis*), Mahua flowers (food, alcohol) and seeds (oil), Bamboo (fences, baskets), Siadi creeper (ropes), Semur bark (grating), various herbs, roots, fruits, gums, resins, hides, horns, silk cocoons, ivory.

5.  **Traditional Conservation:**
    *   **Sacred Groves:** Known by various local names like *sarnas, devarakudu, kan, rai*, protected by communities (e.g., Mizoram to Kerala).
