# [Class 9] History - Chapter 5: Pastoralists in the Modern World

## 🌟 Core Concepts

1.  **Pastoral Nomadism:**
    *   **Definition:** A way of life centred around herding livestock (sheep, goats, cattle, camels) and involving regular, often seasonal, movement between locations to find fresh pastures and water. Nomads do not live permanently in one place.
    *   **Dependence on Environment:** Pastoral life is shaped by geography (mountains, plateaus, deserts) and climate (seasonal changes, rainfall patterns).
    *   **Economic Activities:** Primarily subsistence based on livestock products (milk, meat, wool, skin), supplemented by trade and sometimes small-scale cultivation or crafts (e.g., blanket weaving). Often involves symbiotic relationships with agricultural communities (manuring fields).

2.  **Major Pastoral Communities & Their Movements:**
    *   **India:**
        *   **Himalayas (Transhumance):**
            *   *Gujjar Bakarwals (Jammu & Kashmir):* Goats/Sheep herders. Winter in low Siwalik hills, summer in high Kashmir valley pastures. Move in *kafilas*.
            *   *Gaddis (Himachal Pradesh):* Sheep/Goat herders. Similar cycle: Winter in Siwaliks, summer in Lahul & Spiti, sometimes higher meadows.
            *   *Gujjars (Garhwal/Kumaon), Bhotiyas, Sherpas, Kinnauris:* Cattle/other livestock. Winter in *bhabar* (dry forests), summer in high meadows (*bugyals*).
        *   **Plateaus, Plains, Deserts:**
            *   *Dhangars (Maharashtra):* Primarily shepherds, also blanket weavers/buffalo herders. Monsoon on central plateau (semi-arid), move to Konkan post-monsoon (Oct) for *kharif* stubble/manuring, return before next monsoon.
            *   *Gollas (Karnataka/AP):* Cattle herders.
            *   *Kurumas/Kurubas (Karnataka/AP):* Sheep/Goat herders, blanket sellers.
            *   *Banjara (UP, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP, Maharashtra):* Cattle traders, moved long distances.
            *   *Raikas (Rajasthan):* Camel (Maru Raikas) and Sheep/Goat herders in Thar desert. Stay in villages during monsoon, move out (Oct) seeking pasture/water.
    *   **Africa:**
        *   *Maasai (Kenya/Tanzania):* Cattle herders in East Africa.
        *   *Others:* Bedouins, Berbers, Somali, Boran, Turkana.

3.  **Impact of Colonial Rule (India & Africa):**
    *   **Shrinking Grazing Lands:**
        *   *Waste Land Rules (India):* Uncultivated land (often pastures) declared 'waste', taken over by the state, and given for cultivation to increase land revenue and grow cash crops needed by Britain.
        *   *Forest Acts (India):* Forests classified as 'Reserved' (no access) or 'Protected' (restricted access with permits) to control valuable timber resources (e.g., deodar, sal). Grazing seen as harmful to saplings.
        *   *Land Appropriation (Africa):* Best grazing lands taken for white settlement and agriculture. Large areas turned into Game Reserves (e.g., Maasai Mara, Serengeti Park) barring pastoralists.
    *   **Regulation and Restriction of Movement:**
        *   *Permit System (India):* Required for entering forests, specified timing and duration, fines for overstaying.
        *   *Criminal Tribes Act (India, 1871):* Classified many nomadic communities (pastoralists, traders, artisans) as 'criminal by nature', restricting their movement to notified settlements under police watch. Based on suspicion of mobile populations.
        *   *Territorial Boundaries (Africa):* Imposition of international borders (e.g., dividing Maasailand between British Kenya and German Tanganyika) and internal reserve boundaries, restricting traditional mobility. Permits required, difficult to obtain.
    *   **Increased Economic Pressure:**
        *   *Grazing Tax (India):* Introduced mid-19th C, levied per animal. Collection methods became stricter (initially auctioned to contractors, later collected directly via passes).
        *   *Trade Restrictions (Africa):* Pastoralists often prohibited from entering markets in 'white areas' or participating in trade.

4.  **Consequences for Pastoralists:**
    *   **Ecological:** Serious shortage of pastures, overgrazing of remaining lands leading to deterioration, reduced natural regeneration.
    *   **Economic:** Decline in livestock quality and numbers (especially during droughts/famines due to restricted movement), loss of livelihood, increased indebtedness, forced shift to labour.
    *   **Social:** Disruption of traditional social structures (e.g., Maasai elder/warrior system weakened by British-appointed chiefs), growing inequality between wealthy and poor pastoralists.

5.  **Adaptation and Survival:**
    *   Reducing herd size to match available pasture.
    *   Discovering and changing movement routes (e.g., Raikas moving to Haryana post-Partition).
    *   Diversifying income: Combining pastoralism with cultivation, trade, wage labour (e.g., construction, charcoal burning).
    *   Settling down: Richer pastoralists buying land and becoming settled peasants or traders.
    *   Political Action: Pressing governments for rights, relief, subsidies, and participation in resource management.

6.  **Contemporary Relevance:**
    *   Pastoralism recognized by ecologists as a sustainable livelihood, especially in arid and mountainous regions.
    *   Pastoralists are not relics but active communities adapting to the modern world, facing ongoing challenges regarding land rights and resource access.

## 📘 Key Learnings

*   **Nomadic Pastoralism as an Adaptation:** Understand that nomadic pastoralism is not aimless wandering but a strategic adaptation to environments where agriculture is difficult. Movement is essential for finding pasture and water, dictated by seasons and geography. *Diagram Idea: Map illustrating the seasonal movement (transhumance) of Gujjar Bakarwals between Jammu plains (winter) and Kashmir valley (summer).*
*   **The Logic of Colonial Policies:** Colonial governments viewed pastoral lands and forests primarily through the lens of revenue generation (land tax, timber) and administrative control. Uncultivated land was seen as 'unproductive waste'. Mobile populations were considered suspicious and difficult to rule, leading to restrictive laws.
*   **How Colonial Laws Transformed Pastoral Life:**
    *   **Waste Land Rules:** Directly reduced the area available for grazing by converting pastures into farms.
    *   **Forest Acts:** Denied access to traditional forest pastures, forcing pastoralists onto smaller, often poorer quality grazing lands. The permit system curtailed freedom of movement and traditional resource management. *Diagram Idea: Flowchart showing: Forest Act -> Declaration of Reserved/Protected Forests -> Restricted Access/Permits -> Reduced Pasture Availability -> Overgrazing -> Decline in Livestock.*
    *   **Criminal Tribes Act:** Stigmatized entire communities, severely restricted mobility, and disrupted their livelihoods and social fabric through forced settlement and surveillance.
    *   **Grazing Tax:** Added a significant financial burden, making pastoralism less viable, especially for poorer herders.
*   **The Maasai Case Study (Africa):** Illustrates the profound impact of colonial boundaries and land policies. The arbitrary division of Maasailand, loss of prime grazing areas to settlers and game reserves (like Serengeti), and confinement to arid zones drastically affected their ability to cope with drought, leading to massive livestock losses and social changes (rise of colonial chiefs, growing inequality). *Diagram Idea: Map showing pre-colonial Maasailand vs. post-colonial confinement area including game reserves.*
*   **Coping Mechanisms as Resilience:** Pastoralists were not passive victims. They actively responded to changes by adapting their herding practices (reducing numbers), finding new routes and pastures (Raikas to Haryana), diversifying their economy (combining herding with farming/trade/labour), and engaging politically.
*   **Ecological Significance:** Recognize that traditional pastoral movements often allowed pastures to recover, preventing overuse. In many dry and hilly regions, pastoralism remains an ecologically sound way of life.

## 🌏 Bharatiya Context

*   **Diverse Communities:** India has a rich diversity of pastoral communities adapted to different ecological zones:
    *   **Himalayas:** Gujjar Bakarwals (J&K), Gaddis (HP), Gujjars, Bhotiyas, Sherpas, Kinnauris (Uttarakhand region). Their life revolves around *transhumance* – seasonal migration between high-altitude summer pastures (*bugyals*) and low-altitude winter grazing grounds (like the *bhabar* or Siwalik hills).
    *   **Plateau/Plains/Desert:** Dhangars (Maharashtra), Gollas, Kurumas, Kurubas (Karnataka/AP), Banjaras (multi-state), Raikas (Rajasthan). Their movements are often dictated by the monsoon cycle and relationship with agricultural communities (e.g., Dhangars manuring Konkan fields post-*kharif* harvest in exchange for rice).
*   **Impact of Colonial Laws in India:**
    *   **Waste Land Rules:** Directly impacted Indian pastures, converting them for revenue-generating cultivation.
    *   **Forest Acts:** Severely restricted access to forest resources crucial for grazing, especially impacting communities like Gaddis and Gujjars. Commercially valuable timber like *deodar* and *sal* were prioritized.
    *   **Criminal Tribes Act (1871):** Targeted many mobile Indian communities, leading to stigmatization and forced settlement.
    *   **Grazing Tax:** Imposed a direct economic burden on Indian pastoralists, collected via permits and passes.
*   **Post-Independence Scenario:**
    *   The creation of the India-Pakistan border after 1947 disrupted traditional routes, forcing communities like the Raikas (who previously grazed camels near the Indus in Sindh) to find new pastures, leading some to migrate annually to Haryana.
